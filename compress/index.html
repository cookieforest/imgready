What image compression actually does

Image compression is a family of techniques for reducing the number of bytes a digital image takes up on disk and on the wire. Cameras and phones produce images that are deliberately uncompressed or only lightly compressed so they preserve every pixel for editing. By the time those images are seen on a website, an email, or a social-media feed, their full fidelity is wasted on viewers who are looking at a 800-pixel-wide rendering on their phone. Compression is what closes the gap between "raw archival quality" and "what's actually delivered to a screen".

There are two compression families. Lossy codecs (JPEG, WebP, AVIF, HEIC) keep the image looking nearly identical to the human eye but discard high-frequency detail and fine color variations the eye can't easily distinguish — this is how a 4 MB photo turns into a 200 KB JPG that still looks like the same photo. Lossless codecs (PNG, lossless WebP, lossless AVIF) keep every pixel exactly. They produce smaller savings but are essential for screenshots, line art, logos, and anything with hard edges or transparency.

Why your site will be faster after you compress

Images are usually the largest payload on a typical web page — frequently more than the HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and fonts combined. Modern Lighthouse and Core Web Vitals reports treat the largest image as the Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) target. Halving the size of that image often halves your LCP. The downstream effects compound: faster pages keep more visitors, rank higher in Google, and cost less to serve from a CDN. For an e-commerce site moving 50,000 product photos a month, a 70% size reduction is a meaningful CDN bill cut.

How imgready compresses

imgready uses two pipelines. For JPG, GIF and lossless PNG output, it uses your browser's built-in canvas encoder, which is hardware-accelerated and produces results indistinguishable from professional desktop tools. For WebP and AVIF, it uses jsquash, a set of WebAssembly ports of the reference C++ encoders that ship with Squoosh — the same encoders Google uses internally. For lossy PNG, imgready uses UPNG.js to perform color quantization, producing a PNG-8 file that can be 40–70% smaller than the lossless original at quality settings below 100. Whichever path your image takes, the entire process happens inside the browser tab. Closing the tab is the same as deleting the working data.

A note on the "compress to a target size" question

People often ask "compress to 100 KB" as if there is a magic button. Actual file size is a product of three things — pixel dimensions, content (a photo of grass compresses very differently from a screenshot of text), and quality setting. The reliable way to hit a small target size is: 1) resize the longest side to no larger than your final display dimension; 2) pick the right format (WebP for general web, AVIF for max compression, lossless PNG only for graphics); 3) drop the quality slider to 70 if 82 still produces too-large files. Doing all three usually gets you under any reasonable target without visible quality loss.

Frequently asked questions

Is image compression free?

Yes. imgready is completely free with no limits, no signups, no rate caps.

Will compression reduce my image quality?

With the right settings, compression reduces file size with no visible quality loss. imgready defaults to a quality setting of 82, which is widely considered the sweet spot for most images. You can adjust this higher for maximum quality or lower for maximum compression.

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. All compression happens locally in your browser using JavaScript and WebAssembly. Your images are never transmitted over the internet.

Can I compress multiple images at once?

Yes. Drop as many images as you want into imgready and compress them all with a single click. The soft cap is 200 files; the practical limit is your device's memory.

What is the best format for web images?

WebP offers the best combination of compression and browser support for modern websites. AVIF compresses even more but has slightly less browser support. JPG remains the most universally compatible format for photographs.

Does imgready work on iPhone or iPad?

Yes. imgready works in mobile Safari and Chrome. iOS Safari has known issues with the native WebP encoder, so on iOS we automatically swap to the WebAssembly encoder so output is consistent.
