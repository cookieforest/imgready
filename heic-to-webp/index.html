HEIC → WebP: the modern web's photo pipeline

The story of "iPhone photo on the web" used to involve two steps: first convert HEIC to JPG (because the web couldn't read HEIC), then compress the JPG (because phone photos are far too large). The story today, on a modern browser audience, has a single answer: convert directly to WebP. WebP combines the photographic-friendly compression of JPG with the alpha-channel support of PNG, in a single format that every recent browser decodes natively. For an iPhone photo destined for a blog, an Etsy listing, a Shopify store, a portfolio, or any modern website, HEIC → WebP is almost always the right destination.

Lossy vs lossless WebP for iPhone photos

For photographs, lossy WebP is what you want. imgready's default quality of 82 produces files visually identical to the HEIC source, typically 70–80% smaller than the equivalent JPG. For pixel-perfect copies (the rare case where a photographer wants a lossless web copy), set quality to 100 — imgready outputs lossless WebP, which is typically 26% smaller than the equivalent PNG with identical fidelity.

When NOT to use WebP for iPhone photos

  • Email attachments. Many corporate inboxes still don't preview WebP. Send JPG.
  • Print pipelines. Most print shops still prefer JPG or TIFF. Check before sending.
  • Older devices in your audience. If you support Internet Explorer or pre-2020 Android stock browser, WebP needs a fallback.

If you need to support older browsers, generate the WebP first, then run the same HEIC again with JPG selected — and serve them via the HTML <picture> element with WebP first and JPG as fallback.

How imgready converts HEIC to WebP

HEIC is decoded with libheif-js (WebAssembly) and the resulting raw pixel data is encoded using Google's reference WebP encoder, also compiled to WebAssembly via the jsquash library. Both libraries run entirely in your browser. On non-iOS devices, imgready can also fall back to the browser's native canvas WebP encoder for speed; on iOS Safari (which has known WebP encoding bugs) imgready always uses the WebAssembly path so output is consistent regardless of platform.

Why convert iPhone HEIC photos directly to WebP?

HEIC is already a fairly compressed format, so converting to WebP doesn't give the same dramatic size reduction you'd see with a BMP or TIFF. What the conversion does do is make the file universally usable: HEIC has poor support outside Apple devices, while WebP works in every modern browser and most major platforms. For website use, social media uploads via a browser-based tool, or sharing with Windows users, HEIC to WebP is a clean single-step solution.

HEIC to AVIF: the next step up from WebP

WebP delivers great compression with 97% browser support. AVIF goes further — 20–30% smaller than WebP at equivalent quality, with 93% browser support. If you're already optimizing for modern browsers, consider HEIC to AVIF for maximum compression savings on your iPhone photos.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use HEIC to WebP or HEIC to JPG?

If your audience is on modern browsers (essentially everyone since 2020), WebP. If you're emailing the photo or sharing it with a system that may not support WebP, JPG is safer.

Does WebP work for iPhone photos with lots of fine detail?

Yes — WebP at quality 82 is visually indistinguishable from the source HEIC for almost all photographs. For pixel-perfect output, use lossless WebP (quality 100).

Will my WebP files have transparency?

WebP supports a full alpha channel. Most iPhone photos don't have transparency, but if yours do, WebP preserves them perfectly.

How is the file size compared to original HEIC?

Typically about the same to slightly smaller. WebP at quality 82 produces a file roughly the same size as the HEIC source, sometimes 5–10% smaller. The big win versus JPG is universal browser support without the JPG file-size penalty.

Is HEIC to WebP private?

Yes. The decoder and encoder both run inside your browser. Your photos never leave your device.

Can imgready convert HEIC to WebP in batch?

Yes. Drop dozens or hundreds of HEIC files at once and click Process. The soft cap is 200 files; the practical limit is your device's memory.
