Why 100 KB is the number everyone lands on

100 KB is not an arbitrary round figure — it is roughly the point where an image stops being the slowest thing on a web page. Google's own performance guidance treats images as the single largest contributor to Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), the Core Web Vital that measures when your main content actually appears. On a mid-range phone over 4G, a 100 KB image arrives in a fraction of a second; a 2 MB one does not. That is the whole reason this specific target keeps showing up in CMS upload limits, marketplace listing rules, job-application portals and university submission forms.

The catch is that "compress to 100 KB" is a file size instruction, while every normal image tool gives you a quality slider. Quality 80 might land at 60 KB for one photo and 400 KB for another, because file size depends on resolution and on how much detail the photo actually contains. A flat blue sky compresses to almost nothing; a close-up of gravel does not. So the usual workflow is guesswork: pick a quality, export, check the size, adjust, repeat.

How imgready hits the number instead of guessing

Set the target once and the encoder does the searching. imgready runs a binary search over encoder quality directly in your browser, encoding the image several times at different settings and keeping the highest quality whose output still fits under your ceiling. Because the search is over real encodes rather than an estimate, the result is the actual file you download — not a prediction that turns out wrong.

The rule the tool enforces is deliberately one-sided: the output lands at or under 100 KB, never over. If a form rejects anything above 100 KB, a tool that returns 103 KB has failed you completely, so overshoot is treated as a bug rather than a rounding detail.

Which output format to pick for a 100 KB target

Format matters more than the slider here, because the byte budget is fixed and the only question is how much picture you can buy with it.

JPG — the safe default

Universally accepted by every upload form, email client and printing service. If something else is going to read the file and you do not control what, choose JPG. imgready encodes JPG with MozJPEG, which typically produces files 10–25% smaller than a browser's built-in encoder at the same visible quality — meaning more detail survives inside the same 100 KB.

WebP — noticeably better picture at the same size

WebP is 25–35% more efficient than JPEG, so a 100 KB WebP simply looks better than a 100 KB JPG of the same photo. Support is effectively universal in browsers now. Use it when the destination is a website you control.

Avoid PNG for this particular job

PNG has no quality dial to search over — it is lossless by design, so the encoder cannot trade detail for bytes the way JPG and WebP can. Forcing a photograph into 100 KB of PNG means dropping to a tiny palette and visible colour banding. Keep PNG for screenshots, logos and flat graphics; use JPG or WebP for photographs on a byte budget.

When 100 KB is not reachable — and what to do

Sometimes the target is physically impossible at the image's current dimensions. A 6000 × 4000 photo from a modern camera contains 24 million pixels; even at aggressive compression, encoding that many pixels takes more than 100 KB. When that happens the tool gives you the smallest file it can produce rather than pretending, and the fix is to reduce dimensions first.

This is the step most people skip. Resolution is the strongest lever on file size there is: halving an image's width and height removes three quarters of its pixels before compression even begins. A 1200 px-wide photo at reasonable quality lands under 100 KB comfortably, and 1200 px is already wider than most content columns on the web. Set a resize alongside the size target and the two work together — imgready resizes first, then searches quality against your ceiling.

Rough guide to what fits

As a starting point for photographic content at a 100 KB ceiling: around 1600 px wide is usually achievable for simple scenes, 1200 px is reliable for most photos, and 800 px leaves room to spare even for busy, detailed images. Flat or graphic content goes much further; dense texture — foliage, crowds, gravel, fabric weave — much less.

Nothing is uploaded, including the originals you are shrinking

Every encode runs inside your browser tab using WebAssembly builds of the same encoders professional tools use. Your images are never transmitted anywhere, which matters more than usual for this use case: images destined for job applications, visa forms, insurance claims and medical portals are exactly the files you should not be handing to an anonymous web service. You can verify the claim yourself — open your browser's Network tab and watch it stay empty while the compression runs.

The practical side effects are that it works offline after the first visit, there is no queue or rate limit, and batch size is bounded by your device rather than someone's server bill. Drop up to 200 files, set the ceiling once, and every file comes back under it.

Frequently asked questions

Will the result always be under 100 KB?

Yes, whenever it is achievable at the image's dimensions. The encoder searches quality and returns the best-looking result that still fits at or under 100 KB — it never returns a file over your target. If 100 KB is impossible at the current resolution (a very large photo, for example), you get the smallest file the encoder can produce, and reducing the dimensions will then bring it under.

Which format should I choose to hit 100 KB?

JPG if anything other than a browser will read the file — it is accepted everywhere. WebP if the destination is a website you control, because WebP is 25–35% more efficient and therefore looks better at the same 100 KB. Avoid PNG for photographs on a byte budget: PNG is lossless and has no quality dial, so hitting a small target means heavy colour banding.

Does compressing to 100 KB ruin the quality?

It depends entirely on resolution. At 1200 px wide, most photographs at 100 KB look completely fine for web use. At 6000 px wide, 100 KB is far too little and the image will visibly degrade. The fix is to resize the dimensions down first and let compression work on a sensible pixel count — imgready can do both in the same pass.

Can I compress many images to 100 KB at once?

Yes. Drop up to 200 files, set the target once, and every file is encoded against the same ceiling independently — each one gets its own quality search, because the quality needed to hit 100 KB differs per image. Download them individually or all together as a ZIP.

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. Compression runs locally in your browser using WebAssembly, so the files never leave your device. That is the reason this tool is appropriate for documents you would not upload elsewhere — application photos, ID scans, insurance evidence. You can confirm it by watching the Network tab in your browser's developer tools while a file compresses.

Why does the same quality setting give different file sizes?

Because file size is driven by how much detail the image contains, not by the slider alone. Smooth gradients and flat colour compress extremely well; fine texture like foliage, fabric or gravel does not. This is precisely why targeting a size directly is more useful than targeting a quality number — the tool adapts the quality per image to meet your byte budget.
