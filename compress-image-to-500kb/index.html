Where a 500 KB ceiling makes sense

500 KB is not a web-performance budget — for a single web image it is generous, several times what a well-optimised hero needs. It is a containment budget. The typical uses are attachment limits, CMS media-library policies, ticketing and helpdesk systems, shared drives where hundreds of camera originals are quietly eating storage, and print-adjacent workflows where you want detail preserved but not 12 MB per file.

Camera and phone originals routinely land between 3 and 12 MB. Capping them at 500 KB typically removes 90–95% of the weight while keeping enough detail that the images still look right at full screen. That is the sweet spot this page targets.

Why a ceiling beats a quality setting here

Because file size and quality are not the same axis. Quality 80 might produce 200 KB for a portrait against a plain wall and 2 MB for a photo of dense foliage — same setting, tenfold difference, because compression can only exploit redundancy that exists. If your constraint is "nothing over 500 KB", stating that directly is the only reliable way to satisfy it.

The encoder searches quality with real encodes and keeps the highest-quality result that still fits. Output lands at or under 500 KB, never above.

At this budget, format is the interesting choice

With 500 KB to spend, all the modern formats produce good results, so pick on destination rather than on efficiency.

WebP is the default recommendation for anything shown in a browser: roughly 25–35% more efficient than JPEG, so 500 KB of WebP holds noticeably more detail. AVIF goes further still (often 40–50% below JPEG) and at this budget produces excellent fidelity, at the cost of slower encoding. JPG remains right whenever something outside your control reads the file — email, print, marketplace listings, older business software.

Keep an eye on dimensions

A 500 KB ceiling is easy to hit even at large resolutions, which means it is also easy to keep images far bigger in pixel terms than you actually need. If these files are going on a web page, resizing to the display width will cut the size dramatically and let the encoder spend the remaining budget on fidelity rather than on surplus pixels.

Batch behaviour

This ceiling is most useful applied to a folder rather than a single file. Drop up to 200 images and each gets an independent quality search against the same 500 KB limit — the flat product shot settles high, the busy landscape settles lower, and every result respects the cap. Download individually or as a ZIP.

Everything runs in your browser through WebAssembly, so nothing is uploaded, there is no per-file server limit, and large camera originals cost no transfer time. It also keeps working offline after the first load.

Frequently asked questions

Will the output always be under 500 KB?

Yes whenever it is achievable, which at 500 KB it almost always is. The encoder searches quality and returns the best-looking result at or under the ceiling, never above it. Only an extremely large image might fail to fit, in which case reducing dimensions resolves it immediately.

Is 500 KB small enough for a website?

It is safe but not optimal. For a single hero image 500 KB loads acceptably, but it is several times what a well-optimised web image needs — 100–200 KB is the usual target. Use 500 KB when the constraint is an attachment or upload limit rather than page speed.

Which format should I choose at this budget?

WebP for anything displayed in a browser, since it holds noticeably more detail per byte. AVIF if you want maximum fidelity and can accept slower encoding. JPG whenever the file goes somewhere you do not control, such as email, print or a marketplace listing.

How much will my camera photos shrink?

Typically by 90–95%. Phone and camera originals commonly run 3–12 MB, so a 500 KB cap removes the vast majority of the weight while keeping enough detail to look correct at full screen.

Should I resize as well?

If the images are destined for a web page, yes. A 500 KB ceiling is easy to hit even at very large dimensions, so you can end up keeping far more pixels than you display. Resizing to the display width shrinks the file further and lets the encoder spend the budget on fidelity.

Are the images uploaded anywhere?

No. All encoding happens in your browser using WebAssembly, so large originals cost no upload time and never leave your device. You can verify this in the Network tab of your browser's developer tools.
