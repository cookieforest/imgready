How JPG compression actually works

JPG (also called JPEG) is a 1992 image format that does one thing extremely well: it makes photographs small. Unlike PNG, which preserves every pixel exactly, JPG is a lossy format — it analyses the image, breaks it into 8×8 blocks, applies a discrete cosine transform, and then throws away the high-frequency detail that the human eye is least likely to notice. The result is a file that visually looks identical at typical viewing distance but takes a fraction of the bytes. A 20-megapixel photo straight off a smartphone might be 7 MB at 100% JPG quality and only 600 KB at quality 82 — about a 90% reduction with no visible loss.

The catch is that every time you re-encode a JPG, a tiny amount of additional quality is lost. So if you compress, then later compress again, then again, you'll eventually see "ringing" around hard edges and blocking artefacts in flat areas. The right strategy is to keep one high-quality master copy of every photo (in JPG quality 95+ or in PNG) and compress only the working copies you actually publish. imgready never touches your local file — it makes a separate compressed copy and downloads that — so the master stays untouched.

When to convert JPG to WebP or AVIF instead

JPG is universally supported but it's an old format. WebP, available in every modern browser since 2020, produces files that are typically 25–35% smaller than the equivalent JPG at the same visual quality. AVIF compresses even more — 40–50% smaller than JPG — but at the cost of slightly slower encoding. For any web context (a CMS, a blog, an online store), converting JPG to WebP is the single highest-leverage optimization you can make. imgready can output your images as JPG plus WebP plus AVIF in the same pass, which lets you serve a <picture> element with all three sources and let the browser pick the best one it supports.

EXIF metadata: what it is and whether to strip it

JPG photos from phones and cameras carry an EXIF block — a small chunk of metadata holding the camera model, lens, GPS coordinates of where the photo was taken, exposure settings, and more. EXIF is invisible to the eye but visible to anyone who downloads your image. For social media or any public posting, stripping EXIF is a privacy best practice — your follower base does not need to know your home address. imgready strips EXIF by default; uncheck the toggle in Settings if you specifically want to preserve camera metadata (for example, for portfolio sites where photographers want their EXIF visible).

Common JPG compression mistakes

  • Re-saving JPGs over and over. Each re-encode loses a little quality. Keep an unmodified master.
  • Saving screenshots as JPG. Screenshots have hard edges (text, UI). JPG smears them. Save screenshots as PNG or WebP-lossless instead.
  • Forgetting to resize. A 6000-pixel-wide image displayed at 1200 pixels is wasting most of its bytes. Resize before compressing.
  • Using quality 100 expecting "lossless". JPG is always lossy by design — even at 100. If you need lossless, use PNG or WebP-lossless.

Frequently asked questions

How much can JPG compression actually save?

On a typical phone photo straight from the camera (around 5–8 MB at quality 95), imgready at quality 82 will produce a file 70–80% smaller — usually 1 MB or less — with no visible quality loss at normal viewing distance.

Will JPG compression lose quality every time I save?

Yes — JPG is lossy, so each re-encode loses a tiny amount of quality. Best practice is to keep one high-quality master JPG and compress separate working copies for publication.

Can I batch compress hundreds of JPGs?

Yes. Drop all of them into imgready and click Process. Modern browsers handle a few hundred images comfortably; for larger batches we recommend processing in groups of 100 to avoid memory pressure.

Does compressing JPG strip my GPS coordinates?

Yes — by default, imgready strips EXIF metadata including GPS, camera model and exposure data. If you want to keep this metadata (for example, on a photographer's portfolio), uncheck Settings → Metadata → Remove hidden location & camera data.

Should I use JPG or WebP for my website?

For modern websites, WebP is almost always better — typically 25–35% smaller than the equivalent JPG with no visible quality loss. JPG remains the safest choice when you need universal compatibility (e.g. email attachments).

Why does my JPG look pixelated after compression?

Either the quality is set too low, or the image has been re-compressed multiple times, or it's a screenshot with hard edges that JPG handles poorly. Try quality 85+ or use WebP/PNG instead.
