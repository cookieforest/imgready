When PNG to JPG is the right move

PNG is a lossless format designed for graphics with sharp edges and limited colors — screenshots, icons, logos, illustrations. It is not designed for photographs. When someone (or some camera, or some app, or some download) saves a photograph as a PNG, the result is a file that's typically 4–8 times larger than it would be as a JPG, with quality differences invisible to the eye. Converting these "wrong-format" PNGs to JPG is the highest-leverage compression you can do — savings of 80%+ are typical.

The key tell that you should convert: your PNG is a photograph (continuous-tone color, no sharp edges, no flat regions, no text), and the file is over a few hundred KB. If yes, JPG is almost certainly a better fit.

When PNG to JPG is the wrong move

  • The PNG is a screenshot, logo, or graphic with sharp edges. JPG smears edges and produces visible ringing. Stay with PNG (or convert to WebP-lossless).
  • The PNG has transparency you need to preserve. JPG has no alpha channel. Convert to WebP-lossless instead.
  • The PNG is text-heavy. JPG handles text poorly — letters become fuzzy. Stay with PNG or convert to WebP-lossless.

The transparent-pixel problem and how imgready solves it

JPG cannot represent transparency. When you convert a PNG with transparent areas to JPG without thinking about it, the transparent pixels typically render as solid black — a bad surprise. imgready handles this automatically: when the output format is JPG, the canvas is filled with white before the image is drawn, so transparent pixels become white. This is what almost everyone expects ("white background" is the universal sane default), but if you specifically need a different background color, do the fill yourself in Photoshop or a similar tool first.

Quality settings for PNG to JPG

imgready's default of quality 82 is the universal sweet spot: visually identical to the source PNG at typical viewing distance, with savings of 80–95%. For pixel-peeping use cases (photographs that will be cropped, edited, and re-saved), 90+ is safer. For thumbnails and list previews, 70–75 works.

A worked example

A typical 12-megapixel PNG photograph is around 12 MB. The same image as JPG quality 95 is around 4 MB. As JPG quality 82, around 1.2 MB. As WebP quality 82, around 800 KB. The visual difference between any of these and the source PNG, viewed at normal monitor resolution, is invisible. Converting the PNG to JPG saves over 90% of the file size for free — and converting to WebP saves another 30%.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my PNG photograph so much smaller as a JPG?

PNG is lossless. JPG is lossy and is purpose-built for photographs. For continuous-tone images, JPG can throw away high-frequency detail the eye doesn't notice and end up at a small fraction of the PNG size with no visible difference.

Will PNG to JPG add a black background?

No. imgready fills transparent pixels with white before encoding. If you need a different fill color, edit the PNG in your image tool first.

How much quality am I losing?

At imgready's default quality 82 you're losing data the human eye cannot perceive at typical viewing distance. The original PNG was lossless, so technically yes — pixels change. In practice, you cannot tell.

Can I convert PNG screenshots to JPG?

You can, but you usually shouldn't. JPG handles screenshots poorly — text edges get fuzzy and UI elements smear. Convert to WebP-lossless or PNG-8 instead for a much better result.

Does PNG to JPG keep transparency?

No, because JPG does not support transparency. imgready replaces transparent pixels with white. If transparency matters, convert to WebP instead.

Can I batch convert many PNGs to JPG?

Yes. Drop them all in and click Process. The soft cap is 200 files; the practical limit is your device's memory.
