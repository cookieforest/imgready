Why we built
the image tool that never uploads.

Every other online compressor sends your photo to a server. We didn't want to. Here's the long answer for why imgready works the way it does, and what that means for the next photo you compress.

By Jeffrey · April 2026 · 6 min read

The default people don't think about

Open any popular image compressor. Drop a photo in. Watch what happens. The page shows a progress bar. Bytes are moving. Your photo is travelling across the internet to a server somewhere — typically Amazon, Cloudflare or DigitalOcean. The server does the compression work, then sends a smaller copy back. Most users never notice this is happening because the upload is fast and the result is exactly what they wanted.

This is the default for a reason. Image compression algorithms used to be heavy and complex enough that running them in a browser was unrealistic, so server-side made sense. But that hasn't been true since about 2018. WebAssembly closed the gap. Modern browsers can run the same compression code that runs on a server, locally, fast.

Yet most tools still upload. Why?

Three honest answers

Money. Server-side compression makes the relationship transactional. You give us your file, we give you a service. That's the foundation for accounts, paywalls, monthly limits, paid tiers. TinyPNG charges $39 a year for "more files per day." That makes sense only if there's a server-side queue to ration.

Habit. Most tools were built before WebAssembly was practical. Once the architecture is server-shaped, ripping it out and rebuilding in the browser is months of work. Easier to leave it alone.

Telemetry. When the file flows through a server, the operator gets to see it. Not necessarily in a sinister way — they might just want metadata for analytics. But "we briefly held a copy of your file" is a sentence most companies would prefer not to have to say.

What we built instead

Every part of imgready that touches your image runs in your browser. Specifically:

  • HEIC and HEIF decoding uses a WebAssembly port of libheif — the same C++ library that ships in macOS and iOS.
  • WebP and AVIF encoding use jsquash, the WebAssembly bundles also used inside Squoosh, the open-source compressor by Google's Chrome team.
  • PNG quantization uses UPNG.js running locally.
  • TIFF parsing is hand-rolled JavaScript that handles the three most common compressions (uncompressed, LZW, PackBits).
  • Resizing, cropping and format conversion run on the browser's HTML5 canvas API.

Your photo enters the browser tab, gets decoded into raw pixel data in your computer's RAM, gets re-encoded into your chosen format, and gets handed straight back to the Save dialogue. When you close the tab, every trace is gone. There's no upload progress bar because there is, literally, nothing to upload.

Why this matters past privacy

"My photos stay private" is the obvious benefit. But the architecture also fixes three smaller frustrations that come for free:

Speed. A 5 MB photo on a typical home connection takes 4–6 seconds to upload to a server before processing can even start. imgready sees the same photo instantly. Local compression typically finishes faster than the upload would have.

No file-size caps. TinyPNG caps at 5 MB on the free tier. CloudConvert caps at 1 GB on its $25/month plan. imgready has no caps because there's no server to rent storage on. Drop a 100 MB TIFF if you want.

It's free. Not "free with limits, paid for more." Just free. There's no infrastructure cost on our side because there's no infrastructure. The site runs on Cloudflare's free tier and is paid for by AdSense ads (which don't see your image data) and voluntary Ko-fi contributions.

What we give up

We're not pretending this approach is universally better. Three honest trade-offs:

First load is bigger. Loading the WebAssembly encoders adds about 1.5 MB on first visit (cached after that). A server-side tool can render the page in 100 KB. We think the trade is worth it, but if your bandwidth is precious, fair point.

Mobile gets warm. AVIF encoding is computationally heavy. On a phone it can take 10–15 seconds per image and the device gets noticeably warm. A server can do it in under a second. For batch work on phones, sometimes server-side is just better.

No headless API. Because we run in the browser, you can't curl imgready from a build script. We have an SDK for this — same code that runs on the site, dropped into your own page or app — but it's still a browser-y solution. If you need a backend pipeline, this isn't your tool.

Who this is for

imgready isn't trying to be every image tool. It's specifically for: people who care that their photos don't briefly live on someone else's server; iPhone users who want HEIC photos converted without installing an app; web developers shrinking images before deployment without trusting yet another vendor; anyone whose image is too big for the free tier of a paid tool.

If you're already happy with TinyPNG or Squoosh, that's fine. They're good tools. We just didn't want to build another one shaped exactly like them.

Frequently asked questions

Does imgready ever touch a server?

Your image files never leave your device. The only network requests imgready makes are for the page assets, optional WebAssembly decoders (downloaded once and cached), and ad/analytics scripts — none of which carry your image data.

Can I verify that files aren't uploaded?

Yes. Open your browser's DevTools (F12), go to the Network tab, drop a file, and click Process. You'll see no outbound requests carrying image data.

Why does it matter if images are uploaded?

Professional photos, personal documents, medical images, and business materials carry sensitive information. Uploading them to a third-party server means accepting their privacy policy, storage practices, and data retention. In-browser processing avoids that entirely.

Does imgready strip EXIF data and other metadata?

Yes — imgready removes all metadata including EXIF, IPTC, XMP, and embedded color profiles when converting images. Location data, camera model, timestamps, and other sensitive embedded information are automatically discarded during processing.

Is imgready GDPR compliant?

Yes. Since image processing happens entirely in-browser with no data transmission to servers, GDPR requirements around personal data processing don't apply in the traditional sense. No personal data is collected, stored, or processed server-side.

Could a government force imgready to log or access user images?

No. There is no backend infrastructure that holds image data. The developer cannot access, log, or decrypt user images because they never leave the user's device. The tool has no servers, no databases, and no ability to surveil — making it impossible for third parties to subpoena image data.
