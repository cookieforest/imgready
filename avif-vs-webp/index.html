AVIF vs WebP: Which image format actually wins in 2026?

Both AVIF and WebP beat JPEG on compression. But which one should you actually use for your website, app, or workflow? Here's the full comparison — file size, browser support, encoding speed, quality, and practical recommendations — so you can make an informed decision without the marketing fluff.

Quick answer

If you can only choose one: WebP is the safer, faster, more compatible choice for most websites. If you want the best possible compression and can handle a fallback stack: use AVIF as primary, WebP as fallback. JPEG should be reserved for environments that don't support either.

2026 browser support at a glance: WebP — ~97% global coverage (all major browsers). AVIF — ~93% global coverage (Chrome, Firefox, Edge, Safari 16+). The 4% gap is mostly older Safari and some legacy Android browsers.

Compression: AVIF wins — but not by magic

AVIF is built on the AV1 video codec, which uses significantly more sophisticated compression algorithms than WebP's VP8/VP9 foundation. For photographic content, AVIF typically produces files 20–30% smaller than WebP at equivalent visual quality — and roughly 50% smaller than JPEG.

In practice, a hero image that would be 200 KB as a JPEG might be 140 KB as WebP and 100 KB as AVIF. For a page with 10 images, that's potentially 1 MB saved compared to WebP, and 2 MB compared to JPEG.

Where AVIF's compression advantage is most visible

The gap is largest for high-detail photographs and images with subtle gradients. For flat graphics, logos, and illustrations with large solid color areas, the difference between AVIF and WebP narrows considerably — and lossless PNG may still be the right choice for pixel-crisp clarity.

Browser support: WebP leads, but AVIF is closing the gap

WebP launched in 2010 and achieved near-universal browser support by 2021. AVIF launched in 2020 and hit 93% coverage by 2025. The remaining ~7% gap consists mostly of older iOS Safari versions (pre-iOS 16), legacy Android WebViews, and some enterprise browsers locked to older versions.

For consumer-facing websites where users are on modern devices, AVIF's 93% coverage is more than acceptable. For enterprise or government apps where you can't control browser versions, WebP's 97% is safer.

The right fallback strategy

Use the HTML <picture> element to serve the best format each browser supports:

<picture>
  <source srcset="image.avif" type="image/avif">
  <source srcset="image.webp" type="image/webp">
  <img src="image.jpg" alt="Your image">
</picture>

This stack serves AVIF to Chrome, Firefox, Edge, and modern Safari; falls back to WebP for older Safari; and falls back to JPEG for everything else. Zero compatibility breakage, maximum compression savings.

Encoding speed: WebP is much faster

AVIF encoding is computationally expensive — 5 to 20 times slower than WebP at equivalent quality settings. For pre-processing a library of 10,000 images, this matters significantly. An AVIF conversion that takes 20 minutes would take 1–4 minutes for WebP.

For static websites where images are processed once at build time, this one-time cost is acceptable. For on-the-fly image transformation at scale (e.g., a CDN resizing user-uploaded photos in real time), the encoding overhead of AVIF can add latency or require more compute resources.

Quality at low bit rates

AVIF's biggest advantage over WebP shows up at very low file sizes. When you aggressively compress an image to, say, 50 KB, AVIF preserves much more detail than WebP. WebP tends to produce blocky artifacts at low quality settings; AVIF maintains smoother gradients and more natural-looking compression.

For images where you need the absolute minimum file size without visible quality loss — mobile hero images, thumbnails, catalog photos — AVIF is the stronger choice.

When to use AVIF

  • High-quality photographs where maximum compression matters
  • Hero images, product photos, and editorial imagery on modern websites
  • When you can implement a WebP/JPEG fallback via <picture>
  • HDR photography or content with wide color gamut requirements
  • Pre-built static sites where encoding happens once at build time

When to use WebP

  • When you need maximum browser compatibility without managing fallbacks
  • Real-time image processing pipelines where encoding speed matters
  • Animated images (WebP animates well and is universally supported)
  • Flat graphics, logos, and UI assets where AVIF's advantage is minimal
  • CMS uploads where you want simplicity over absolute compression

The practical 2026 recommendation

Use AVIF when…

  • Your site targets modern users
  • You can add a WebP fallback
  • Images are pre-processed, not dynamic
  • Bandwidth savings are critical
  • You have photographic content

Use WebP when…

  • You need maximum compatibility
  • Images are generated on the fly
  • You’re optimizing animated GIFs
  • Simplicity matters more than file size
  • You’re migrating from JPEG today

Frequently asked questions

Is AVIF better than WebP?

AVIF achieves better compression than WebP — typically 20–30% smaller files at equivalent quality. However, WebP has broader browser support (97% vs 93%) and encodes much faster. For most websites in 2026, the best practice is to serve AVIF as the primary format with WebP as a fallback.

Should I use AVIF or WebP for my website?

Use both with the HTML <picture> element: serve AVIF to browsers that support it, and fall back to WebP for the rest. This gives you maximum compression without compatibility gaps. If you can only pick one, WebP is the safer choice with its 97% browser coverage.

Does Safari support AVIF?

Yes. Safari added full AVIF support in Safari 16 (released with iOS 16 and macOS Ventura in 2022). As of 2026, all modern Safari versions support AVIF. Older devices running iOS 15 or earlier will not display AVIF — one more reason to implement a WebP fallback.

How much smaller is AVIF compared to JPEG?

AVIF files are typically 50% smaller than equivalent-quality JPEG files. WebP is approximately 30% smaller than JPEG. So AVIF offers roughly 20–30% additional savings over WebP for the same image. At very low quality settings (aggressive compression), AVIF's advantage grows even larger.

What is the downside of AVIF?

AVIF's main downsides are slower encoding speed (5–20× slower than WebP) and slightly lower browser coverage (93% vs WebP's 97%). For static images on a pre-built site this is rarely a problem, but for dynamic image generation at scale, the encoding overhead can be significant. Also worth noting: AVIF doesn't support progressive loading like JPEG does.
