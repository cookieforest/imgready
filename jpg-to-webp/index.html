Why JPG to WebP is the highest-ROI conversion

If you run a content website, an online store, a portfolio, or anything image-heavy, the single biggest performance lever is converting your JPGs to WebP. WebP at quality 82 is visually indistinguishable from a JPG at quality 90 but produces a file 25–35% smaller. On a page with ten images, that's a 2–3 MB transfer saving per visitor. For a site doing 100,000 page views a month, that's 200–300 GB of bandwidth saved at the CDN — and the visitors get a noticeably faster experience.

When not to convert JPG to WebP

  • Email attachments. Many corporate email pipelines still don't decode WebP previews.
  • Photo printing services. Most still want JPG.
  • Old browsers. Internet Explorer can't read WebP. If your audience is in that bucket, serve a JPG fallback.

For everything else — blog hero, product photo, portfolio image, social-share OG image, app screenshot, even your favicon — WebP is the better choice.

Setting up <picture> with WebP and JPG fallback

If you support older browsers, the safe pattern is to serve WebP first and JPG as a fallback inside an HTML <picture> element. The browser picks the first format it can decode:

<picture>
  <source srcset="/photo.webp" type="image/webp">
  <img src="/photo.jpg" alt="..." width="1200" height="800">
</picture>

Quality settings for JPEG to WebP conversion

WebP quality settings don't map 1:1 to JPEG quality. A JPEG at quality 85 looks roughly equivalent to WebP at quality 80 — WebP's compression is more efficient so you can use a lower number to get the same visual result. For web images, WebP quality 75–82 is the sweet spot: significantly smaller than the source JPEG, with no visible quality loss at typical screen viewing sizes. For print-quality output or archival use, WebP quality 90+ is safer.

Frequently asked questions

Will JPG to WebP look the same as the original?

At imgready's default quality of 82, the WebP output is visually indistinguishable from the JPG at normal viewing distance. The savings come from WebP's more efficient compression, not from quality loss.

How much smaller will my files be?

Typically 25–35% smaller for already-compressed JPGs, much more (often 80%+) for high-quality JPGs straight from a camera.

Will old browsers display the WebP?

Browsers shipped since 2020 will. For older browsers (Internet Explorer, pre-2020 Android stock), use the element to serve a JPG fallback.

Can WebP have transparency?

Yes — WebP has a full alpha channel. For converting JPG to WebP this rarely matters (JPGs don't have transparency), but it makes WebP a great PNG replacement separately.

Should I just always use WebP for everything on my website?

For modern audiences, yes. For email and print, stay with JPG. The simplest universal answer is to generate both and use the element.

Does converting JPG to WebP lose any more quality?

Technically yes — re-encoding from one lossy format to another adds a small amount of additional loss. At quality 82 it is invisible. To minimize the loss, stay at quality 85+ when re-encoding from a high-quality source.
