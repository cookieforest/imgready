WebP vs PNG vs JPG
which format wins, and when

A practical, up-to-date comparison of the three image formats most websites actually deal with — what they do well, what they don't, and how to pick the right one for any given image. Plus a quick decision tree at the end.

The short answer

  • Photographs on a modern website? WebP at quality 82.
  • Photographs you need to email? JPG at quality 82.
  • Screenshots or UI? WebP-lossless (quality 100), or PNG-8 if compatibility is critical.
  • Logos, icons, vector-y graphics? SVG if available, otherwise WebP-lossless or PNG.
  • Anything with transparency? WebP or PNG. Never JPG.
  • Maximum compression with full browser support? AVIF on modern browsers (separate guide on /avif-converter/).

The rest of this page explains why each of those answers is what it is.

Format-by-format

JPG / JPEG

JPG (the file extension) and JPEG (the format) are the same thing. Created in 1992, refined over three decades, supported by every operating system, every browser, every email client, every camera, every printer, and every piece of consumer software ever shipped. JPG is a lossy format optimised for photographs — it analyses the image, breaks it into small blocks, and discards high-frequency detail the human eye is less likely to notice. The result is a small file that looks identical to the source at typical viewing distance.

Where JPG wins: universal compatibility. If you're not sure what software the recipient has, send JPG.

Where JPG loses: no transparency support; visible artefacts on flat colors and sharp edges (text, UI, logos); slight quality loss every time you re-save; bigger files than WebP at the same quality.

PNG

PNG (1996) was invented as a free replacement for the patent-encumbered GIF. It's a lossless format — every pixel is preserved exactly. PNG supports a full alpha channel (transparency) and works in every browser since around 2003. There are two flavours: PNG-24 (full color) and PNG-8 (palette quantized to 256 colors), the latter producing files 40–70% smaller for content with limited color palettes.

Where PNG wins: screenshots, logos, icons, line art, anything with sharp edges or transparency. Pixel-perfect at any quality setting. Universal compatibility.

Where PNG loses: photographs (it's lossless, so the file is enormous compared to JPG); no good way to compress further without color quantization; slightly bigger than WebP-lossless for the same content.

WebP

WebP (2010, Google) is the modern web's default format. It supports both lossy and lossless modes in the same file format, plus full alpha channel, plus animation. Browser support reached 100% of major browsers in 2020. WebP-lossy is typically 25–35% smaller than the equivalent JPG; WebP-lossless is typically 26% smaller than the equivalent PNG; both at the same visual quality.

Where WebP wins: almost everywhere on the modern web. Smaller than JPG for photos, smaller than PNG for graphics, full alpha channel, universal modern browser support.

Where WebP loses: some non-browser pipelines (email previews, print shops, older design tools) still don't decode WebP. For those, you need a JPG or PNG fallback.

Decision tree

  1. Is the image content photographic (continuous-tone, no sharp edges)?
    • Yes → is the destination an email or non-browser pipeline?
      • Yes → JPG at quality 82.
      • No → WebP at quality 82 (or AVIF if you really care about the last 30%).
    • No (it's a screenshot, logo, icon, illustration) → does it need transparency or sharp edges preserved?
      • Yes → WebP-lossless (quality 100). Or PNG-8 if you specifically need PNG compatibility.
      • No → WebP-lossy at quality 82 still works, often beats PNG-8 on size.
  2. Do you need to support pre-2020 browsers (Internet Explorer, very old Android)?
    • Yes → generate WebP, then run the same image again with JPG (or PNG) selected to produce the fallback, and serve both via the HTML <picture> element.
    • No → just serve WebP.

What about AVIF?

AVIF is the next-generation format, typically 20–30% smaller than WebP for the same visual quality. Browser support reached majority share in 2023. The trade-off is encoding time — AVIF encodes 5–10x slower than WebP. For static assets you compile once and serve forever, AVIF is worth it. For real-time encoding (e.g. user uploads), WebP is still the better default. We have a longer take on AVIF on the AVIF converter page.

Practical answer for most websites

If you don't want to think about it: generate WebP for everything, with a JPG fallback for photos and a PNG fallback for graphics, served via the <picture> element. Drop the same image into imgready twice — once for WebP, once for the fallback — and you have a complete set ready to drop into the markup.

What about AVIF?

AVIF is the newest entry in the next-gen format competition, and in 2026 it's a serious contender. It compresses 20–30% better than WebP at equivalent quality, supports HDR and wide color gamut, and has ~93% browser support. For a detailed comparison of AVIF and WebP specifically, see the AVIF vs WebP comparison guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is WebP really always better than JPG?

For photographs displayed on a modern web browser, yes — typically 25–35% smaller at the same visible quality. For email or non-browser destinations, JPG is still safer because of broader compatibility.

Should I just use AVIF for everything?

AVIF compresses tighter than WebP but encodes 5–10x slower and has slightly less universal browser support. For static assets compiled once, AVIF is fine. For dynamic content, WebP is a better default.

Can I serve WebP and have old browsers fall back to JPG?

Yes. Use the HTML element with a WebP source first and a JPG fallback. Browsers that support WebP use it; older browsers fall back automatically.

What about PNG-8 vs WebP-lossless?

Both are lossless options for graphics. WebP-lossless is typically 26% smaller than PNG-24 for the same content. PNG-8 (color-quantized) can be smaller still for very flat illustrations but introduces visible color banding on photographs.

Is AVIF supported by Safari?

Yes — Safari has supported AVIF since macOS Ventura (2022) / iOS 16. Total browser support is ~95%+ as of 2026. For production, still use a fallback in the element.

Why would I ever pick JPG today?

Compatibility. Email clients, photo printing services, and a handful of older or specialised pipelines still expect JPG. If your audience or destination might fall in those categories, JPG is the safe choice.
