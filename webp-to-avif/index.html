Implementing AVIF on your website

Once you have your AVIF files, use the HTML <picture> element to serve them with a WebP fallback, this way browsers that don't yet support AVIF automatically get the next-best format:

<picture>
  <source srcset="hero.avif" type="image/avif">
  <source srcset="hero.webp" type="image/webp">
  <img src="hero.jpg" alt="Hero image">
</picture>

This stack covers ~97% of browsers without serving a single unnecessarily large file. Browsers try each source in order and use the first they support.

Keep your originals: Converting WebP → AVIF involves two lossy compression steps. For the best results, convert from the original JPEG or PNG source directly to AVIF when possible. Use imgready's JPG to AVIF or PNG to AVIF converters for single-step quality.

When the two-step WebP → AVIF conversion is acceptable

If you no longer have the original JPEG or PNG files and only have WebP versions, converting WebP to AVIF directly is perfectly fine for most use cases. The quality difference from a double-pass compression is subtle and imperceptible at quality settings of 85+ on AVIF. You'll still gain meaningful file size savings over keeping the WebP files as-is.

Frequently asked questions

Why convert WebP to AVIF?

AVIF achieves 20-30% better compression than WebP at equivalent quality. If you already converted your images from JPEG to WebP, upgrading to AVIF is the natural next step for maximum web performance. The switch is especially worthwhile for high-traffic image-heavy pages where every KB of savings multiplied across millions of requests adds up.

Does converting WebP to AVIF lose quality?

Both WebP and AVIF use lossy compression, so a two-step conversion does apply compression twice. To minimize quality loss, use the highest quality setting available (90+) when converting. For truly lossless output, AVIF's lossless mode preserves every pixel, though file sizes will be larger than lossy AVIF. The best approach when quality is critical is to convert from the original uncompressed source directly to AVIF.

Is AVIF supported in all browsers in 2026?

AVIF has approximately 93% global browser coverage as of 2026, supported by Chrome, Firefox, Edge, and Safari 16+. The remaining ~7% is mostly older iOS Safari (pre-iOS 16) and legacy enterprise browsers. Use a WebP fallback via the HTML <picture> element to ensure your images display correctly for everyone.

How much smaller is AVIF compared to WebP?

AVIF files are typically 20-30% smaller than WebP files at equivalent visual quality. For a 200 KB WebP image, the AVIF version would be approximately 140-160 KB. The savings are most pronounced for photographic content with gradients and textures, and least for flat illustrations and graphics with large solid-color areas.

Should I keep the original WebP files after converting to AVIF?

Yes. Keep your WebP files as fallbacks. When deploying AVIF on your site, use the HTML <picture> element to serve AVIF to browsers that support it and WebP to the rest. This covers 97%+ of browsers without serving unnecessarily large files to older clients.
