JPG vs PNG — what's the difference and when does it matter?

JPG and PNG are the two workhorses of image formats. They look similar until you need transparency, small files, or lossless edits — then the differences matter. This guide explains when to use each, why file sizes differ, and whether "quality" even applies the way you think it does.

The quick answer

JPG: photographs, social media, email attachments, web backgrounds. Small files, good for anything you won't edit again. No transparency.

PNG: screenshots, logos, icons, graphics with transparency, anything you plan to edit. Lossless, but larger files. Universal browser support.

If you need transparency or sharp text, use PNG. If you need small files for a photograph, use JPG. If you want the smallest file possible, use WebP (which does both). The rest of this guide explains why.

What is JPG?

JPG (or JPEG — the terms are interchangeable) was created in 1992 and has dominated photograph distribution ever since. It's a lossy compression format, meaning it analyzes your image, identifies frequencies the human eye is unlikely to notice, and throws them away. The algorithm is remarkably good — a JPG compressed to 50% of its original file size often looks identical to the uncompressed original.

JPG is called lossy because data is discarded. Every time you re-save a JPG (edit it and save it again), more data gets thrown away, and the image degrades slightly. Open a JPG in Photoshop, make a small edit, and save it again as JPG, and you've added a new generation of compression artifacts. This is why JPG is a distribution format, not an editing format.

JPG strengths: tiny files for photographs, supported by absolutely every device and software since 1992, looks good even at high compression. JPG weaknesses: no transparency, visible artifacts on sharp edges and text, destructive re-saving, not suitable for editing workflows.

What is PNG?

PNG (Portable Network Graphics) was created in 1996 as a free, unpatented replacement for GIF. It's a lossless format — every pixel is preserved exactly. There are two variants: PNG-24 (full 16.7-million color), which is what you get from most editors, and PNG-8 (palette quantized to 256 colors), which is much smaller but only suitable for flat illustrations.

PNG supports an alpha channel (transparency), making it perfect for logos, icons, and screenshots. You can save a PNG, edit it in Photoshop, save it again, and the second save is bitwise identical to the first — no quality loss, no artifacts. This makes PNG the preferred editing and archival format.

PNG strengths: lossless, full transparency support, no quality loss on re-save, sharp edges, editing-friendly, universal browser support. PNG weaknesses: large files for photographs (4–8x bigger than JPG), overkill if you're not editing or using transparency.

When to use JPG

JPG is the right choice when file size matters and you don't need transparency or lossless editing. Use JPG when:

  • Photographs for the web. JPG is 4–8x smaller than PNG and looks visually identical for photo content.
  • Social media uploads. Instagram, Facebook, Twitter — all compress heavily anyway, and JPG is smaller than PNG.
  • Email attachments. JPG is smaller and universally supported. PNG works too, but the file is unnecessarily large.
  • Web backgrounds and banners. Photographic backgrounds stay small as JPG.
  • News articles and blogs. Photographs are JPG; simple graphics are PNG.
  • When you won't edit again. JPG is final — once compressed, there's no recovering lost detail. If you're done with the image, JPG is fine.

When to use PNG

PNG is the right choice when transparency, sharp edges, or lossless editing matters. Use PNG when:

  • Logos and brand marks. Need transparency to layer over backgrounds, and edges must stay pixel-perfect.
  • Icons and interface elements. Sharp edges, full transparency, typically small dimensions so file size isn't a concern.
  • Screenshots. Text needs to stay sharp, and JPG artifacts make text blurry. PNG preserves every pixel.
  • Anything with transparency. Cutout images, subjects on transparent backgrounds, layered graphics — PNG is the only option.
  • Editing source files. Work in PNG if you plan to edit and re-save. JPG re-compression is destructive.
  • Graphics with flat colors or vector-y art. PNG-8 (palette quantized) is tiny for illustrations and stays sharp.
  • Charts, diagrams, and infographics. Sharp edges, limited color palettes, PNG-8 can be very small.

Does PNG look better than JPG?

This is the most-asked question, and the answer is nuanced: it depends on the content. PNG is lossless and JPG is lossy, but that doesn't automatically make PNG "better quality." Here's why:

For photographs: JPG at quality 85 looks identical to PNG to the human eye, but JPG is 6–8x smaller. The tiny loss of high-frequency data is imperceptible for continuous-tone images like photos. PNG is not "better" — it's just bigger and lossless, which is useful for editing, not viewing.

For screenshots and text: PNG is sharper because JPG's compression algorithm introduces artifacts around hard edges. Text in a JPG looks blurry; in PNG it's crisp. Here, PNG is objectively "better" for the content.

For logos and icons: PNG with flat colors (or PNG-8) is tiny and perfect. JPG would introduce banding and fuzzy edges. PNG wins.

So the real answer is: format choice depends on content type, not on abstract notions of quality. JPG is optimal for photos, PNG for everything else. Both formats look great at their intended use cases.

Quality loss on re-saving

This is where the "editing" difference becomes critical. JPG uses lossy compression, meaning data is thrown away. If you open a JPG, make a tiny edit (change one pixel), and save it as JPG again, the compression algorithm analyzes the image fresh and throws away different high-frequency data, potentially creating visible artifacts or banding.

PNG is lossless, so every save is pixel-perfect. Edit a PNG a hundred times and the 100th save is still bitwise identical to the original. This is why PNG is the editing format and JPG is the delivery format.

File size comparison: why PNG is bigger

A typical photograph saved as JPG (quality 85) might be 500 KB. The same photograph as PNG could be 2.5–4 MB. The difference is fundamental:

  • JPG: Analyzes the image, discards high-frequency data the eye won't miss, encodes the remaining data compactly. Result: 50–70% size reduction with imperceptible quality loss for photos.
  • PNG: Losslessly compresses every pixel. Uses filtering and LZ77-style compression, but can't throw away data. For photographs (continuous-tone, lots of variation), there's not much to compress without losing quality. Result: large files.

That's why PNG is great for flat illustrations (limited color palettes compress well) and terrible for photographs (every pixel is unique, no compression wins). JPG is the opposite — excellent for photos, but creates artifacts on sharp edges and flat colors.

Frequently asked questions

Is PNG better quality than JPG?

PNG is lossless and JPG is lossy, so PNG preserves every pixel perfectly. However, that doesn't make PNG 'better quality' — it depends on your content. For photographs, JPG looks identical to PNG at a fraction of the file size. For text, logos, and screenshots, PNG preserves sharp edges while JPG creates artifacts. PNG is better for editing (no re-compression loss) but not necessarily better for viewing.

Does JPG support transparency?

No. JPG has no alpha channel and cannot represent transparent pixels. If you need transparency, you must use PNG, WebP, or another format that supports an alpha channel. If you convert a JPG to PNG, the conversion preserves the image but doesn't magically add transparency — you'd need to edit out the background manually.

Which format is better for printing?

For photographs, JPG is the standard and is universally supported by print services. For graphics, logos, and artwork with transparency, PNG is better. Most professional print workflows prefer either uncompressed formats (TIFF) or high-quality JPG. When in doubt, ask your printer or use their online uploader to test.

Can I convert JPG to PNG without quality loss?

The conversion itself is lossless — every pixel in the JPG is preserved in the PNG. However, if the JPG is already compressed and lossy, no conversion can recover the detail the JPG discarded. PNG will hold that image steady for future edits, but it won't make a blurry JPG sharp. PNG is useful if you plan to edit further without re-compression, not for restoring lost detail.

Why are PNG files so much bigger than JPG?

JPG uses lossy compression that discards high-frequency detail (especially in smooth areas like skies and skin tones), reducing file size dramatically. PNG is lossless — it keeps every pixel. For photographs, a PNG can be 4–8x larger than a JPG at similar perceived quality. If file size matters, stay with JPG for photos or use WebP for even better compression.

Which format should I use for my website?

Photographs: JPG or WebP (WebP is smaller and increasingly supported). Screenshots, icons, logos, anything with transparency: PNG or WebP-lossless. For maximum compatibility with old browsers, serve both formats via the HTML <picture> element — WebP first for modern browsers, JPG/PNG as fallback. For 2026, WebP is the best all-around choice if browser support isn't a concern.
