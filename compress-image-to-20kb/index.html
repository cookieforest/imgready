Why 20 KB is different from the other targets

At 100 KB you can usually keep a normal web-sized photograph and simply let compression do its work. At 20 KB you cannot. Twenty kilobytes is roughly the size of a paragraph of formatted text, it is not enough to describe a large grid of pixels at any quality. So the question stops being "how hard should I compress?" and becomes "how many pixels can I afford?".

This is the target that appears in the strictest upload forms: scanned signatures, exam-portal photographs, banking KYC uploads, older government systems, and avatar or profile-picture slots in legacy software. Those systems were built when bandwidth was scarce and the limits were never revisited.

What actually fits in 20 KB

For a signature: dark strokes on white: 20 KB is generous, because plain white compresses to almost nothing. For a headshot on a plain background, a portal-specified size such as 200 × 230 px fits comfortably. For a general photograph, expect to land around 300-500 px on the long edge; beyond roughly 600 px the artefacts become obvious. A busy, detailed scene at 20 KB will look poor at any resolution, because there is simply nowhere to put the detail.

The practical order of operations is therefore: set the dimensions the form asks for (or the smallest you can accept), choose JPG, then apply the 20 KB ceiling. imgready resizes first and then searches encoder quality against the target, so the two settings cooperate.

Read the form's rules carefully first

Strict portals rarely specify only a maximum. Look for exact pixel dimensions, a minimum file size (some reject anything under 10 or 20 KB, which means you may need to aim just below the ceiling rather than as small as possible), an accepted format list that is usually JPG-only, and occasionally a required background colour. Hitting the maximum while violating one of the others still gets you rejected, which is where most of the frustration comes from.

Use JPG, not PNG

PNG is lossless and has no quality dial for the encoder to search, so forcing a photograph into 20 KB of PNG means cutting the palette until the image visibly posterises. JPG is what these portals expect and what their validators accept, and imgready encodes it through MozJPEG, which fits measurably more detail into a small budget than a browser's built-in encoder, which matters a great deal when the budget is this tight.

The exception is a signature or line drawing: pure black on white with few colours is exactly the content PNG-8 handles well, and it can be both tiny and perfectly crisp. If the form accepts PNG for a signature, that is often the better choice.

If it will not fit

When the target is unreachable at the current dimensions you get the smallest file the encoder can produce rather than a false success. The fix is always the same and always works: reduce the pixel dimensions. Halving width and height removes three quarters of the pixels before compression even begins, which is far more effective than pushing quality lower on an oversized image.

If you have already resized to the form's required dimensions and still cannot fit, which happens with detailed colour photographs at strict limits, consider whether the subject can be cropped tighter. A head-and-shoulders crop contains far less information than a full scene, and cropping is lossless.

Your ID documents never leave the device

This matters more here than anywhere else in the toolset, because of what these files are: passport photographs, signature scans, identity documents, exam admit cards. Uploading those to an unknown compression site means handing identity documents to a third party with no accountability.

imgready never transmits them. The encoders are WebAssembly builds running in your own browser tab; the file is read from disk locally and the result written back locally. Open the Network panel in developer tools and you will see no image traffic while it works. It also keeps functioning offline after the first load, which is useful in an office queue with poor signal, and you can process a whole family's or class's worth of photos in one batch.

Frequently asked questions

Is 20 KB even possible for a photo?

Yes, but resolution decides it. A signature or a headshot on a plain background fits comfortably. A general photograph needs to come down to roughly 300-500 px on the long edge; beyond about 600 px the artefacts become obvious. A busy, detailed scene will look poor at 20 KB regardless of settings.

What should I set the dimensions to?

Whatever the form specifies: that always wins. If it does not say, aim for 300-500 px on the long edge for a photograph, or the portal's typical headshot size such as 200x230 px. Set the dimensions first, then apply the 20 KB ceiling, because resizing changes what quality is needed.

JPG or PNG at this size?

JPG for photographs: it is what strict portals expect and MozJPEG fits more detail into a tiny budget. PNG only for a signature or line drawing, where pure black on white compresses extremely well as PNG-8 and stays perfectly crisp.

The form also has a minimum file size. What then?

Aim just under the ceiling rather than as small as possible. Some portals reject files under 10 or 20 KB as well as those over the maximum, so a very aggressive compression can fail validation for the opposite reason. Nudge the target up until you sit between the two limits.

It still will not fit. What else can I do?

Crop tighter. Reducing dimensions is the first lever, but a head-and-shoulders crop simply contains less information than a full scene, and cropping is lossless, so it buys real headroom that compression cannot.

Is it safe to compress ID documents and signatures here?

The files are never uploaded. Compression runs inside your browser using WebAssembly, so identity documents, signatures and application photos stay on your device. You can verify it by watching the Network tab in developer tools while a file compresses.
