Best image format for email

JPEG is the right choice for photographs. It offers excellent compression for photographic content and is universally supported across every email client — including Outlook 2003, Apple Mail, Gmail, and every mobile client.

PNG is better for screenshots, diagrams, logos, and any image with large flat-color areas or text. PNG is lossless, so text remains crisp, but PNG files are larger than JPEGs for photographic content.

Avoid AVIF and WebP in email: Despite being excellent formats for the web, AVIF and WebP have poor support in email clients. Outlook (Windows) does not render WebP or AVIF inline, and many other clients will show a broken image icon. Stick with JPEG or PNG for attachments and HTML email images.

What about HEIC?

HEIC files should always be converted before emailing. While macOS and iOS can display HEIC, Windows and Android email clients typically cannot. Convert first with imgready's HEIC to JPG tool — no software to install.

How much can you compress a photo without visible quality loss?

At JPEG quality 80–85, most photographs compress to 15–25% of their original size with no perceptible quality loss when viewed at normal email sizes (100–600px wide on screen). A 6 MB smartphone photo becomes 400–900 KB — well within any email limit.

At quality 70, you can push further (files 10–15% of original) with very subtle quality loss visible only on close inspection at full resolution. For most email use cases, quality 75–82 is the sweet spot.

Resizing vs compressing

Resizing (reducing pixel dimensions) and compressing (reducing quality) both reduce file size. Resizing first is more efficient — a 4000×3000 photo resized to 1600×1200 is already 75% smaller before any compression. Then compressing gives you another 60–75% reduction. Combined, you can take an 8 MB photo to under 300 KB with no visible difference at email display sizes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the ideal image size for email attachments?

For email attachments, aim for under 1 MB per image, with a total email size under 10 MB. Most email clients and servers reject messages over 25 MB. For inline email images (embedded in HTML emails), keep each image under 200 KB for fast rendering on all devices.

What image format is best for email?

JPEG is the best format for photographic email attachments — it offers the best compression-to-quality ratio and is supported everywhere, including Outlook. PNG is better for screenshots, logos, and graphics with text. Avoid AVIF and WebP in email — Outlook and many other clients can't display them.

How do I compress an image for email without losing quality?

Use imgready's free in-browser compressor: drop your image, set quality to 80–85 for photos or 90 for graphics, and download the compressed version. At quality 82, most JPEGs reduce by 60–80% with no visible quality difference at normal email viewing size.

Does Gmail have a file size limit for email attachments?

Yes. Gmail limits total message size to 25 MB. However, for reliable delivery across all email providers, keep your total attachments under 10 MB — many corporate mail servers enforce a 10 MB cap. For large files, share a Google Drive or Dropbox link instead.

Can I send multiple images in one email?

Yes, but watch the total attachment size. If you need to send 10 photos, compress each one to under 500 KB first, keeping the total under 5 MB. For large photo sets, consider a cloud sharing link (Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud) rather than direct attachments.

Should I resize images before emailing them?

Yes, absolutely. A 4000×3000 pixel photo from a modern smartphone contains far more resolution than anyone needs to view in email. Resize to 1200–1600 pixels wide before compressing — this alone reduces file size by ~75%. Use imgready's resize tool, then compress the result for maximum reduction.
