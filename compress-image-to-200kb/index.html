200 KB is the quality-first budget

Where 50 KB is a rule imposed by a form and 100 KB is a performance guideline, 200 KB is usually a deliberate choice: you want the image to look genuinely good and you are willing to spend bytes on it. It is the natural budget for a full-width hero image, a blog post's featured image, product photography where detail sells the item, and portfolio work where compression artefacts would be embarrassing.

It is also still fast. 200 KB over a normal mobile connection arrives quickly enough that it will not be the bottleneck in your Largest Contentful Paint, provided it is the only large asset competing for bandwidth. The trade is deliberate: roughly double the bytes of a 100 KB image in exchange for meaningfully more detail, especially on wide or texture-heavy photographs.

What the extra headroom actually buys

Two things, and it is worth choosing which one you want. You can spend it on resolution — keeping a 2000 px-wide image instead of shrinking to 1200 px, which matters for hero banners on large displays and for images users may zoom. Or you can spend it on fidelity at the same dimensions, which matters for photographs full of fine texture: foliage, hair, fabric weave, crowds, architectural detail. Those subjects show compression artefacts first, and 200 KB gives the encoder enough room to keep them clean.

What you should not do is leave the dimensions enormous and rely on compression alone. A 5000 px photo squeezed into 200 KB looks worse than a 1800 px version of the same photo at the same file size, because the encoder is forced to discard detail across four times as many pixels. Resolution first, then the byte budget.

How the target is met

Set 200 KB as the ceiling and imgready searches encoder quality by running real encodes — not estimates — and keeping the highest-quality result that still fits. Because it is a genuine search over actual output, the file you download is the file that was measured. The result lands at or under 200 KB and never above it.

In practice a 200 KB ceiling usually resolves to a fairly high quality setting for normally-sized web images, which is exactly the point: you are not asking for aggressive compression, you are asking for a predictable maximum. That predictability is what makes it useful at scale — a whole gallery encoded to the same ceiling gives you a page whose total image weight you can actually calculate in advance.

Format choice when quality is the priority

WebP — the best picture per byte

At a quality-first budget, WebP is the strongest choice for anything displayed in a browser. It is 25–35% more efficient than JPEG, so a 200 KB WebP carries noticeably more detail than a 200 KB JPG of the same photograph. Browser support is effectively universal now.

AVIF — smaller still, slower to encode

AVIF compresses tighter again, often 40–50% below JPEG, which at 200 KB means very high fidelity. The cost is encoding time — AVIF is built on the AV1 video codec and does substantially more work, which is noticeable in the browser on large batches or older hardware. For a handful of hero images compiled once, it is an excellent trade.

JPG — when something else has to read it

If the file is going into an email, a print workflow, a marketplace listing or any system you do not control, use JPG. At 200 KB, JPG through MozJPEG produces genuinely good results for typical web dimensions — this budget is comfortable rather than tight.

PNG remains the wrong tool for photographs

PNG has no quality dial for the encoder to search, being lossless by design. At 200 KB a photograph would need its palette cut down until banding appears. Use PNG for screenshots, UI graphics, logos and anything with text or hard edges, where losslessness is the actual requirement.

Batch a whole gallery to one ceiling

The real value of a size target appears at volume. Drop up to 200 files, set 200 KB once, and every image gets an independent quality search against that ceiling — the flat-background product shot will settle at a high quality, the dense forest photograph at a lower one, and both come back under budget. That is the outcome a single global quality setting cannot give you, because identical quality settings produce wildly different file sizes depending on image content.

All of it runs in your browser through WebAssembly encoders, so nothing is uploaded, there is no queue, and the only limit on batch size is your own device's memory. Results download individually or together as a ZIP.

Frequently asked questions

Is 200 KB small enough for good page speed?

For a single hero or featured image, yes — 200 KB loads quickly on a normal mobile connection and will not usually be the bottleneck in Largest Contentful Paint. Where it becomes a problem is volume: ten 200 KB images on one screen is 2 MB. Use 200 KB for images that carry the page and a tighter budget for thumbnails and list views.

Should I choose WebP or JPG at 200 KB?

WebP if the image is displayed in a browser you control — it is 25–35% more efficient, so 200 KB of WebP visibly beats 200 KB of JPG. JPG if the file is going anywhere you do not control, such as email, print or a marketplace listing, because compatibility matters more than efficiency there.

Will the output ever be larger than 200 KB?

No. The encoder searches quality and keeps the best result that stays at or under your ceiling, so overshoot does not happen. If 200 KB is unreachable at the image's current dimensions, you receive the smallest file it can produce and reducing the resolution will bring it under.

Is it better to resize or to compress harder?

Resize first, almost always. Halving width and height removes three quarters of the pixels before compression starts, which preserves far more apparent sharpness than keeping a huge image and compressing it aggressively to the same file size. Set the dimensions you actually need, then apply the byte ceiling.

Can I use AVIF for a 200 KB target?

Yes, and at this budget AVIF produces excellent fidelity because it compresses roughly 40–50% tighter than JPEG. The trade-off is encoding speed — AVIF derives from the AV1 video codec and takes noticeably longer in the browser, which is worth considering on large batches or older devices.

Does every image in a batch get its own quality setting?

Yes. Each file is searched independently against the same 200 KB ceiling, which is the point of targeting size rather than quality: a flat product shot and a dense landscape need very different quality settings to arrive at the same file size, and the tool works that out per image.
