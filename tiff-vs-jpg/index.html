TIFF is the archival standard — lossless, enormous files, professional scanners and cameras. JPG is the web standard — lossy, tiny files, universal compatibility. This guide explains when to use each, how they compare, and how to convert between them.

Quick answer: which one should you use?

If you're archiving, editing, or printing professionally, use TIFF. It preserves every pixel, supports 16-bit color depth, and is the standard in pre-press and photography workflows. If you're sharing, emailing, or publishing to the web, use JPG. It's small enough to attach and email, opens on any device, and is the format the web is built on. If you need both — which most professionals do — keep your master in TIFF and export to JPG for sharing.

What is TIFF and when did it start?

TIFF (Tagged Image File Format) was created in 1986 as a flexible, lossless format for professional photography, print, and scanning. Today it remains the gold standard for archival. Nearly every flatbed scanner defaults to TIFF. Professional cameras offer TIFF as an output option. Print shops, designers, and restoration labs all expect TIFF as the primary deliverable because it preserves full fidelity without compromise.

The defining characteristic of TIFF is that it can store images in multiple ways: uncompressed (largest), LZW-compressed (lossless, smaller), or with alpha channels, 16-bit color, multiple layers, and rich metadata. This flexibility comes at a cost — file sizes are massive. A single 24-megapixel uncompressed TIFF easily reaches 100 MB or more.

When you're stuck with a TIFF file

TIFF shows up in your workflow for predictable reasons. A flatbed scanner produces TIFF by default. A professional photographer shoots RAW, processes in Lightroom or Capture One, and exports a master TIFF for archival. A print shop sends you a high-resolution TIFF proof. A medical imaging system (MRI, CT) exports TIFF. Document scanning workflows produce multi-page TIFF stacks. In all these cases, TIFF is the right choice upstream, but once you need to share or publish, conversion becomes essential.

What is JPG and why it dominates the web

JPG (or JPEG, "Joint Photographic Experts Group") was standardized in 1992 and immediately became the format of choice for web, email, and digital photography because it aggressively compresses images to a fraction of their original size. A 24-megapixel scene that would be a 100 MB TIFF becomes a 2 MB JPG with invisible quality loss. This file-size advantage is why JPG is everywhere — it made image sharing practical on slow internet connections in the 1990s and remains practical today.

JPG uses lossy compression, meaning some information is thrown away during encoding. This is intentional and tunable — you pick a quality slider (usually 75–95) that balances file size against visual fidelity. At quality 80–85, the difference from the original is imperceptible to the human eye at typical viewing distances. At quality 95, the file is nearly as large as a PNG and you gain little.

Why JPG doesn't support transparency

TIFF can store an alpha channel (transparency). JPG cannot. This is because JPG's lossy compression algorithm treats pixels in isolation and has no concept of partial transparency. If you need to preserve transparent areas, convert to PNG instead of JPG. If your TIFF has transparency but you need to share it as JPG, you'll have to composite it against a solid background or use PNG as an intermediate.

When to use TIFF

TIFF is the right choice whenever you need to preserve quality without compromise or when professional workflows expect it. Use TIFF when archiving master images, submitting files to a print shop, editing in Photoshop or a professional RAW processor, or storing scanned documents for legal compliance. Once work is complete, export to JPG or PNG for distribution.

Archival and long-term storage

Museums, archives, libraries, and companies doing document scanning use TIFF as their archival standard. It's lossless, well-supported by tools decades old, and resistant to bit rot. Once a photo is scanned to TIFF, it's preserved. Converting to JPG for the archive is inappropriate — you'd be throwing away quality you can never recover.

Professional print workflows

When you send files to a print shop, lab, or pre-press team, they expect TIFF. Print vendors optimize for TIFF because it guarantees fidelity from monitor to final output. Some labs accept JPG, but not all. Always ask your printer — if they say "TIFF," they mean it, and submitting JPG instead may result in lower quality or outright rejection.

Editing master files

Photographers and designers keep their master files in TIFF (or PSD) and export working copies as JPG. This protects against generation loss — if you edit a JPG, save it, and edit again, quality degrades each time. TIFF, being lossless, never degrades. Work in TIFF (or RAW), finalize your edits, then export to JPG for sharing.

When to use JPG

Use JPG any time you need to share, email, publish, or display an image on the web. It's the format the internet was built on, supported everywhere, and creates files small enough to email without hesitation. If you don't have a specific reason to use TIFF (archival, printing, professional editing), JPG is the safe default.

Email and messaging

Never attach a TIFF to an email unless the recipient specifically asks. JPG opens in any email client, on any phone or computer. TIFF may not. For file-size reasons alone, JPG is always the right choice for email.

Web publishing and blogs

Websites use JPG (or WebP for better compression). TIFF has no place on the web — browsers don't display it natively, and file sizes make pages crawl. Always convert TIFF to JPG or WebP before uploading to a website.

Social media and instant messaging

Instagram, Facebook, Twitter, WhatsApp, Slack — all expect JPG (or PNG, WebP, HEIC). If you try to upload a TIFF, the platform will either reject it or automatically convert it (often with poor results). Always pre-convert to JPG.

Can you convert TIFF to JPG without losing quality?

This question contains a subtle trick. Technically, TIFF is lossless and JPG is lossy, so conversion always loses data. But in practice, this loss is invisible. At JPG quality 80–85, the file is 1/50th the size of a TIFF and the difference is imperceptible at typical viewing distances (monitor, phone, print at standard sizes). For pixel-perfect work or fine-art prints larger than 2 feet, the difference may matter. For everything else, it doesn't.

If you need to preserve every bit of detail, don't convert to JPG — stay with TIFF or use PNG (which is lossless but larger than JPG). But if you're sharing, emailing, or publishing, JPG quality 80–85 is a safe, sensible choice.

The quality slider explained

Most JPEG encoders offer a quality setting from 0–100. Quality 50 produces tiny files with visible artifacts. Quality 75 looks good on web screens. Quality 85 is nearly imperceptible from the source. Quality 95+ is diminishing returns — the file barely shrinks and you might as well use PNG. For most use cases, quality 80–85 is the sweet spot.

Frequently asked questions

How much smaller is a JPG than a TIFF file?

Dramatically smaller. A 24-megapixel TIFF is typically 100–150 MB uncompressed. The same image as a JPG is usually 1–4 MB — a 25–100x reduction. A 50 MP medium-format TIFF can reach 250 MB; as JPG it's 5–12 MB. JPG's lossy compression is ruthlessly effective, which is why it dominates the web and social media.

Does converting TIFF to JPG lose quality?

Yes, technically, because TIFF is lossless and JPG is lossy. But the human eye rarely perceives the difference at JPG quality 80–85 and typical viewing distances. For pixel-perfect archival, stay with TIFF or convert to PNG. For sharing, printing, and web use, JPG quality loss is invisible.

Which format should I use for printing?

TIFF is the professional choice. Print shops, pre-press teams, and labs expect TIFF because it preserves full fidelity without compression. JPG is acceptable for casual lab printing but may show quality loss on large prints or fine-art work. Always ask your print vendor — they may request TIFF specifically.

What's the best format for web and social media?

JPG is the standard. It strikes the right balance between file size and visual quality for web browsers and social platforms. WebP is even more efficient if your audience uses modern browsers (90%+ in 2025). Avoid TIFF entirely for web — very few browsers can display it natively, and file sizes make pages load slowly.

Can Photoshop open TIFF files?

Yes, absolutely. Photoshop is one of the best tools for TIFF — it handles uncompressed, LZW, and PackBits compression, as well as 16-bit and 32-bit color depth. Professional photographers and designers use TIFF in Photoshop as their editing master, then export to JPG, PNG, or WebP for delivery.

Do email clients support TIFF attachments?

Most email clients can receive TIFF attachments without error, but recipients may not be able to open them unless they have a dedicated TIFF viewer installed. JPG, by contrast, opens in any mail client, phone, and web browser. For email attachment sharing, always use JPG or PNG — never TIFF.
