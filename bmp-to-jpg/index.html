Why BMP files are so large

BMP (Bitmap) is one of the oldest image formats — it stores every pixel as raw RGB values with no compression at all. A 4000×3000 pixel image stored as BMP is exactly 4000 × 3000 × 3 bytes = 36 MB. There's no quality to protect; there's simply no compression being applied. BMP was the native format for Windows GDI before better alternatives existed, and it persists today mostly as the default output of certain legacy tools, scanner software, and screen capture utilities on older Windows systems.

JPG, by contrast, applies perceptual lossy compression. The same 4000×3000 image at JPG quality 82 is typically 1.5–3 MB — a 90%+ reduction with no visible difference at normal viewing distance. For anything you want to share, email, upload, or store long-term, JPG is the right format for photographic BMP content.

BMP to JPG vs BMP to PNG

JPG is the right conversion target when your BMP contains photographic content (natural scenes, faces, gradients). Choose PNG instead if your BMP is a screenshot, a diagram, or an image with hard edges and flat colour blocks — JPG's compression introduces subtle blurring around hard edges that PNG avoids by being lossless. If you're unsure, JPG is the better default: it produces much smaller files and handles photographic content well.

What quality setting to use

imgready defaults to quality 82, which is the optimal trade-off for photographic content: visually indistinguishable from the original at normal viewing distance, with maximum file size reduction. If you're converting BMP files that will be printed or displayed on high-DPI screens at large sizes, quality 90 is safer. For thumbnails and previews, quality 70 is fine.

Note: BMP does not support transparency. Unlike PNG or AVIF, there's no alpha channel in a standard BMP, so no transparency information is lost during conversion to JPG (which also doesn't support transparency).

Common sources of BMP files

BMP files typically come from a handful of sources: Windows Paint (which saved BMP by default until Paint 3D was introduced), older scanner software that exports raw bitmap scans, legacy CAD and technical drawing applications, screen capture tools on enterprise Windows systems locked to older software, and some industrial/embedded device outputs. If you're dealing with a batch of BMP files, it's likely they came from one of these sources and converting them to JPG will immediately make them usable everywhere.

Frequently asked questions

Why are my BMP files so large?

BMP stores every pixel as raw data with no compression. A standard 24-bit BMP at 3000×2000 pixels is 18 MB regardless of what the image looks like. JPG applies compression that reduces this to 1–2 MB with imperceptible quality loss.

Will converting BMP to JPG lose quality?

JPG is lossy, so yes — a tiny amount of precision is discarded. At imgready's default quality 82, the output is visually identical to the BMP at normal screen viewing. For archival purposes, consider PNG (lossless) instead.

Can I convert multiple BMP files at once?

Yes. Drop a folder of BMPs and click Process All. All files are converted in sequence in your browser and downloadable as a single ZIP.

My BMP is from a scanner — should I use JPG or PNG?

If the scanned document has text, use PNG — it preserves hard edges and fine text detail losslessly. If it's a photograph, JPG is fine and will produce a much smaller file.

Does imgready support 32-bit BMP with an alpha channel?

imgready reads the image data from the BMP via the browser's built-in canvas decoder. Most modern browsers support 24-bit and 32-bit BMP. If your BMP is an unusual variant (OS/2 BMP, 16-colour BMP), it may not decode correctly.

Can I convert BMP screenshots to JPG?

Absolutely. BMP is the default format for Windows screenshots saved via the Snipping Tool or Print Screen. Converting them to JPG typically reduces the file from 3–6 MB down to 200–400 KB — perfect for attaching to emails or uploading to bug trackers without hitting file-size limits.
