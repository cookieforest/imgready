Why resize before you publish

Resizing is the most under-appreciated optimization. A modern smartphone produces 12-megapixel photos, which is roughly 4000 pixels on the long side. The hero image on your blog is rendered at 1200 pixels wide. Serving the 4000-pixel original means delivering eleven times more pixels than the visitor's screen will ever display. The browser will still resize the image to fit the page — but only after downloading every one of those wasted bytes. Resizing the source down to the actual display dimension cuts file size by 80–90% before you've even thought about format or quality.

The right resize target depends on where the image is going. For a full-width blog hero on a desktop, 1600 pixels on the long side is generous. For an Instagram post, 1080 pixels is the platform's own native resolution. For an email signature, 600 pixels is universally safe. For a social media share preview (the og:image meta tag), 1200×630 is the standard. imgready ships presets for all of these so you don't have to remember the numbers.

How imgready resizes

imgready uses the browser's HTML5 Canvas API with high-quality bilinear interpolation. The longest-side input is the limit; the shorter side is calculated to preserve the original aspect ratio exactly. If you also pick a crop ratio (1:1, 4:3, 16:9, 9:16), imgready first crops from the centre to that aspect ratio, then resizes the cropped result to your target longest side. Cropping always keeps the centre of the image — the most likely place for the subject to be. If the subject is off-centre and you need a different crop, do the crop in your image editor first and let imgready handle resize-and-compress only.

Resize + compress in one click

Resizing and compression are two separate operations, but they pair extremely well. The recommended workflow is:

  1. Set the longest side to your final display dimension (or use a preset).
  2. Optionally apply a crop ratio if your platform requires a specific aspect ratio.
  3. Pick the output format — WebP for general web, JPG for compatibility, PNG for transparency.
  4. Drop the quality slider to 82 (the sweet spot).
  5. Drop your files in. imgready does all four operations in a single pass and gives you the final file ready to download.
Reminder: imgready never increases image dimensions. Resize is one-way: original or smaller. If you have a tiny image and want it larger, you need an upscaler (or just leave it alone — bilinear upscaling looks bad).

Frequently asked questions

Will resizing change my image's aspect ratio?

No. imgready always preserves the aspect ratio when resizing. If you need a specific aspect ratio (1:1, 16:9, etc), use the Crop Ratio buttons in Settings — those crop first from the center, then resize.

Can imgready upscale (make images larger)?

No. imgready only resizes down. Bilinear upscaling produces blurry results; if you need to enlarge, use an AI upscaler designed for that task.

What's the difference between resize and crop?

Resize keeps the whole image visible but makes it smaller. Crop discards parts of the image to fit a specific aspect ratio. imgready can do both in one pass — crop first, then resize the cropped result.

Why does my resized image look blurry?

Two common causes: (1) the original was already small, so resizing to a similar size changes very little; (2) you've upscaled, which imgready does not do — check that your target dimensions are smaller than the original.

Can I resize and compress at the same time?

Yes. Open Settings, set the longest side, then pick your output format and quality. imgready does all three operations (resize → optional crop → compress) in a single pass.

Does resize work on HEIC iPhone photos?

Yes. imgready decodes HEIC/HEIF in-browser and then applies your resize and format choice. The output can be WebP, JPG, PNG, or AVIF.
